Case 02 · Operating model and delivery system

Team Dev Flow + E.X.P.E.R.T.

Agent speed required a new team model—not just a better coding prompt.

The challenge

As AI agents accelerated implementation, the team’s old division of work became a bottleneck. Projects moved through analysts, developers, and reviewers as separate queues, while accountability for the outcome became diffuse. Giving everyone an AI tool was not enough; both the operating model and the delivery controls had to change.

The solution has two connected layers. E.X.P.E.R.T. defines who owns a project from problem to outcome. Team Dev Flow turns accepted scope into bounded implementation, independent review, and evidence for a specific code version.

E.X.P.E.R.T. — the team model

I co-authored the methodology with a colleague who served as project manager. I set the overall direction, core approach, and major corrections; my co-author developed detailed mechanics and worked with AI agents on the documentation. The methodology is captured in an 80-page document.

  • E — End-to-End Ownership: one person is accountable from problem to outcome.
  • X — Cross-functional Execution: AI helps the owner cover the core project lifecycle.
  • P — Project–Person Fit: assignments reflect project complexity and demonstrated capability.
  • E — Expert Network: experts support specific risks without taking over accountability.
  • R — Risk-based Review: review depth follows the consequence of failure.
  • T — Transparent Growth: growth is evaluated through increasing responsibility and result quality.

The model changes project assignment, support, review, learning, and onboarding. It does not erase expertise: an owner leads the project, while experts strengthen the areas where the risk exceeds that owner’s current capability.

Team Dev Flow — the execution layer

I defined the product problem, workflow states, role boundaries, Task Contract, stop rules, and acceptance standard for Team Dev Flow. AI agents implemented the plugin; I controlled scope, verification, and acceptance for each version.

Jira remains the source of agreed scope. A versioned Task Contract records acceptance criteria, non-goals, risks, checks, and authority. A Builder implements only that scope. Separate Verifier and Reviewer roles examine the required evidence and consequential defects against the contract and exact commit SHA. Any new change invalidates the prior review. Merge, deployment, and infrastructure changes remain separate actions that require explicit human authority.

E.X.P.E.R.T. therefore answers “who should own this project and what support do they need?” Team Dev Flow answers “how do we move an accepted task to evidence-backed acceptance?”

Pilot

From 1 to 14 August 2026, five people — me, a project manager, an analyst, and two developers — used Codex and Team Dev Flow. The team completed 12 full cycles across two internal projects, split approximately evenly.

No merge conflicts were recorded during those two weeks. GitLab data for the preceding six months showed two to six per month, averaging about five. This is an early observation, not a proven effect of the plugin alone: accountability, project assignment, review, learning, and onboarding changed at the same time.

The team continued using the workflow without close supervision after the pilot. A stronger conclusion requires a longer observation window; company-wide rollout remains a plan rather than an achieved result.

Evidence boundary

Internal Jira issues, branches, contracts, and GitLab data are protected by NDA. A public Universal edition can demonstrate the workflow on a synthetic project, but it has not yet completed this corporate pilot. Connecting unrestricted MCP servers to sensitive work systems was explicitly rejected.

Evidence basis and limits

Internal project data

The pilot combined a new operating model, Codex, and Team Dev Flow. Internal project artifacts remain private; the unpiloted Universal edition can support a synthetic walkthrough.
